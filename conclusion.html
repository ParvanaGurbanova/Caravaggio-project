Conclusions

This project investigated how Caravaggio's The Calling of Saint Matthew is represented in the ArCo knowledge graph and explored how Large Language Models (LLMs) can support knowledge graph enrichment. By progressively refining our SPARQL queries, we moved from a broad thematic search to the accurate identification of Caravaggio's works through identity-based filtering. We then analysed the RDF description of the artwork and identified several semantic gaps, including missing commissioning information, multilingual labels, and explicit depicted-subject relationships. The LLM experiments showed that both ChatGPT and Gemini were able to recover this missing contextual information, highlighting their potential as complementary tools for enriching cultural heritage knowledge graphs.

Challenges

One of the main challenges was formulating effective SPARQL queries and understanding the structure of the ArCo ontology. We also encountered author ambiguity when searching for "Caravaggio," as several historical figures share the same surname. Another difficulty was determining whether missing information resulted from limitations in the dataset or from the ontology itself.

Solutions

To overcome these challenges, we progressively refined our queries and replaced text-based author filtering with identity-based filtering using Caravaggio's unique IRI. ASK queries allowed us to systematically verify the presence or absence of specific semantic information. For the identified gaps, we applied few-shot prompting with ChatGPT and Gemini to extract commissioners, English title variants, and depicted subjects, demonstrating that LLM-generated knowledge can complement the existing ArCo ontology when carefully verified.

Future Improvements

Future developments could further enrich ArCo by explicitly representing commissioning information, expanding multilingual labels, and modelling depicted subjects using existing ontology classes and properties. More generally, integrating SPARQL-based gap detection with LLM-assisted knowledge extraction and human validation could provide an efficient workflow for improving the completeness and discoverability of cultural heritage knowledge graphs.
